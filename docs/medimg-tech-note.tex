\renewcommand{\abstractname}{Summary}
\begin{abstract}
\noindent
\texttt{medimg} is an open-source Python package for image reconstruction
from undersampled data in two settings: accelerated magnetic resonance
imaging (MRI), where part of k-space is sampled, and sparse-view computed
tomography (CT), where few projection angles are measured. It provides the
forward operators and their exact adjoints, both normalized to unit norm, and
three reconstruction methods behind one interface: Tikhonov regularization
(conjugate gradients), total variation (Chambolle--Pock), and wavelet
compressed sensing (FISTA). This note documents the operators and algorithms
as implemented, reports the verification results (dot-product tests,
operator norms, orthogonality of the wavelet transform, golden-output tests)
and the reconstruction results on test images, gives guidance on choosing
the noise model and the regularization parameters, and states the
limitations. A step-by-step tutorial is provided separately as
\code{examples/00\_getting\_started.ipynb}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ================================================================== %

Both MRI and CT acquire linear measurements $y = Ax + \eta$ of an image $x$.
Acquiring fewer measurements shortens MRI scans and reduces CT dose, but
makes $A$ non-invertible, and the direct reconstruction (the zero-filled
inverse Fourier transform, or filtered back-projection) shows aliasing or
streak artifacts. Compressed sensing~\cite{CandesRombergTao2006,Donoho2006}
shows that an image that is sparse in some representation can be recovered
from incoherent undersampled measurements by solving a regularized problem
that promotes sparsity; Lustig et al.~\cite{LustigDonohoPauly2007} applied
this to MRI, and Sidky and Pan~\cite{SidkyPan2008} to CT with total
variation.

\texttt{medimg} implements this setting for two-dimensional single-channel
images. Its design is deliberately small: every reconstruction function takes
the forward operator \code{A} and its adjoint \code{At} as plain Python
functions, so the same solvers serve MRI, CT, and any operator a user
writes. It depends on NumPy, SciPy, Matplotlib, scikit-image, and
PyWavelets.

\paragraph{Installation.} From the repository root,
\code{pip install -e ".[dev]"} installs the package with the test and
notebook dependencies; \code{uv sync --extra dev} reproduces the exact
environment recorded in \code{uv.lock}. The tutorial
\code{examples/00\_getting\_started.ipynb} covers both modalities step by
step and is executed by continuous integration on every change.

\section{Forward models}
% ================================================================== %

Images are real arrays $x\in\mathbb R^{n_r\times n_c}$ with the Euclidean
inner product. Both operators are linear, have an exact adjoint, and are
normalized so that $\|A\|=1$; regularization parameters are therefore on
comparable scales for the two modalities.

\subsection{MRI}

\paragraph{Operator.} Let $\F$ be the unitary two-dimensional discrete
Fourier transform with the zero frequency at the centre of the array
(\code{fftshift(fft2(ifftshift(x), norm="ortho"))}), and
$M\in\{0,1\}^{n_r\times n_c}$ a sampling mask. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mri}
  A_{\mathrm{MRI}}\,x = M\odot\F x, \qquad
  A_{\mathrm{MRI}}^*\,y = \operatorname{Re}\bigl(\F^{-1}(M\odot y)\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $\odot$ is the entrywise product and k-space carries the real inner
product $\operatorname{Re}\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$
(\code{mri\_forward}, \code{mri\_adjoint}). Because $\F$ is unitary and $M$
a projection, $\|A_{\mathrm{MRI}}\|=1$, and $A_{\mathrm{MRI}}^*$ applied to
the data is the zero-filled reconstruction.

\paragraph{Sampling mask.} \code{generate\_cartesian\_mask(shape,
sampling\_rate, calib\_lines, rng)} fully samples the \code{calib\_lines}
central columns of k-space, which hold most of the energy, and then samples
individual locations outside that band, without replacement, with a
probability proportional to $(1-d^2)^2$, where $d$ is the distance of the
column from the centre column relative to half the width. The total number of
samples is \code{sampling\_rate} times the number of pixels, so the
acceleration factor is $1/\code{sampling\_rate}$. Because the random part
selects points rather than whole lines, the mask corresponds to a 3-D
Cartesian acquisition with two phase-encoding directions; single-slice 2-D
MRI acquires whole lines (see Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{Noise.} \code{mri\_forward(x, mask, noise\_std, rng)} adds
complex Gaussian noise whose real and imaginary parts are independent
$\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$, at the sampled locations only:
$y = M\odot(\F x + \eta)$. Unsampled locations stay exactly zero. Because
$\F$ is unitary, $\sigma$ is on the scale of the image intensities. To
prescribe the data signal-to-noise ratio instead, take
\begin{equation}\label{eq:snr}
  \sigma = \frac{\operatorname{rms}\bigl((\F x)|_M\bigr)}
                {\sqrt2\;10^{\mathrm{SNR}/20}} ,
\end{equation}
as the MRI example and the tutorial do.

\subsection{CT}

\paragraph{Operator.} For parallel-beam CT the forward operator is the Radon
transform $\R$, sampled at angles $\theta_1,\dots,\theta_{N_\theta}$ in
$[0,\pi)$ and at $n$ unit-width detector bins for an $n\times n$ image.
\texttt{medimg} discretizes it as a sparse matrix with a pixel-driven
scheme: each pixel centre inside the inscribed circle is projected onto the
detector, $s = x\cos\theta + y\sin\theta + \lfloor n/2\rfloor$, and its value
is split between the two nearest bins with linear-interpolation weights.
The geometry matches \code{skimage.transform.radon(image, circle=True)}: on
the Shepp--Logan phantom the two sinograms differ by 1.3\% (relative), owing
to the different interpolation. The back-projection is the matrix
transpose, so the pair is exactly adjoint. With \code{normalize=True} (the
default) both \code{ct\_forward} and \code{ct\_adjoint} divide by the largest
singular value of the matrix, computed once with
\code{scipy.sparse.linalg.svds}, so that $\|A_{\mathrm{CT}}\|=1$; with
\code{normalize=False} the forward operator returns line integrals in pixel
units, as filtered back-projection (FBP) expects. Matrices are cached for the
eight most recent combinations of image size and angles.

Version~0.2.0 replaced the earlier operator pair, which applied
\code{skimage.transform.radon} and \code{iradon} with a fixed normalization:
\code{iradon} scales the back-projection by $\pi/(2N_\theta)$, and the two
functions are not discrete adjoints of each other, so the dot-product ratio
was 17.6, 40.8, and 86.6 for 30, 60, and 180 angles. Likewise, the earlier
MRI operator used the unnormalized FFT with the normalized inverse, which made
its adjoint wrong by a factor of $n_rn_c$.

\section{Reconstruction methods}
% ================================================================== %

All three functions take \code{A}, \code{At}, the data \code{y}, a
regularization parameter, and the image \code{shape}. Where a step size is
needed, the Lipschitz constant $L=\|A\|^2$ is estimated by 30 power
iterations of $A^*A$ from a random start (\code{rng} sets the seed) unless
\code{L} is given.

\subsection{Tikhonov regularization}

\code{tikhonov\_cg(A, At, y, lambd, shape, x0, max\_iter, tol)} solves
\begin{equation}
  \min_x\ \tfrac12\|Ax-y\|^2 + \tfrac\lambda2\|x\|^2
\end{equation}
by conjugate gradients on the normal equations
$(A^*A+\lambda I)x = A^*y$, stopping after \code{max\_iter} iterations or
when the residual has fallen below \code{tol} times its value at the
previous iteration. For MRI it barely improves on the zero-filled image: it
cannot recover frequencies that were not measured, apart from those fixed by
the conjugate symmetry of a real image.

\subsection{Total variation}

\code{tv\_chambolle\_pock(A, At, y, alpha, shape, L, max\_iter, theta,
rng)} solves
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tv}
  \min_x\ \tfrac12\|Ax-y\|^2 + \alpha\,\|\nabla x\|_{2,1}, \qquad
  \|\nabla x\|_{2,1} = \sum_{i,j}\sqrt{(\nabla_1x)_{ij}^2+(\nabla_2x)_{ij}^2},
\end{equation}
where $\nabla$ takes forward differences with periodic boundary conditions,
by the primal-dual method of Chambolle and Pock~\cite{ChambollePock2011}
applied to $K=[A;\nabla]$ (Algorithm~\ref{alg:cp}). Since
$\|\nabla\|^2\le8$, the steps $\tau=\sigma=0.99/\sqrt{L+8}$ satisfy
$\tau\sigma\|K\|^2<1$. The dual variable for the data term has the data's
type (complex for MRI, real for CT). The method runs exactly
\code{max\_iter} iterations; there is no stopping test.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{Chambolle--Pock for~\eqref{eq:tv} (\code{tv\_chambolle\_pock})}
\label{alg:cp}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $A$, $A^*$, $y$, $\alpha>0$, $L\ge\|A\|^2$, $\theta\in[0,1]$
\State $\tau=\sigma=0.99/\sqrt{L+8}$; $x^0=\bar x^0=0$; $p^0=0$; $q^0=0$
\For{$k=0,1,\dots,\code{max\_iter}-1$}
  \State $p^{k+1} = \bigl(p^k + \sigma(A\bar x^k - y)\bigr)/(1+\sigma)$
  \Comment{prox of the conjugate of $\tfrac12\|\cdot-y\|^2$}
  \State $q^{k+1} = \Pi_\alpha\bigl(q^k + \sigma\nabla\bar x^k\bigr)$
  \Comment{pointwise projection onto $\{|q_{ij}|\le\alpha\}$}
  \State $x^{k+1} = x^k - \tau\bigl(A^*p^{k+1} - \operatorname{div}q^{k+1}\bigr)$
  \State $\bar x^{k+1} = x^{k+1} + \theta(x^{k+1}-x^k)$
\EndFor
\State \Return $x^{\code{max\_iter}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Wavelet compressed sensing}

\code{cs\_fista(A, At, y, W, Wt, lambd, shape, L, max\_iter, tol, rng)}
solves
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cs}
  \min_x\ \tfrac12\|Ax-y\|^2 + \lambda\|(Wx)_{\mathrm{detail}}\|_1
\end{equation}
by FISTA~\cite{BeckTeboulle2009} (Algorithm~\ref{alg:fista}), where $W$ is a
two-dimensional wavelet transform and only the detail coefficients are
penalized. \code{wavelet\_forward} and \code{wavelet\_adjoint} use periodic
extension (\code{mode="periodization"}), so for an orthogonal wavelet
(Haar, Daubechies, symlets, coiflets) and image sides divisible by
$2^{\text{level}}$ the transform is orthogonal and
$W^*\mathcal S_{\lambda/L}W$ is the exact proximal map of the penalty, as
FISTA requires~\cite{DaubechiesDefriseDeMol2004}. FISTA stops when the
relative change of the iterate falls below \code{tol}.

Version~0.2.0 introduced the periodic extension. With PyWavelets' default
symmetric extension, the db4 transform of a $128\times128$ image has 21\,904
coefficients and is not orthogonal ($\|Wx\|/\|x\| = 1.097$), so the
thresholding step was only an approximation of the proximal map; the Haar
transform was unaffected.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{FISTA for~\eqref{eq:cs} (\code{cs\_fista})}
\label{alg:fista}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $A$, $A^*$, $y$, $W$, $W^*$, $\lambda>0$, $L\ge\|A\|^2$
\State $x^0=z^0=0$, $t_0=1$
\For{$k=0,1,\dots,\code{max\_iter}-1$}
  \State $x^{k+1} = W^*\,\mathcal S_{\lambda/L}\bigl(W(z^k -
  \tfrac1L A^*(Az^k-y))\bigr)$
  \Comment{soft-threshold detail coefficients}
  \State $t_{k+1} = \tfrac12\bigl(1+\sqrt{1+4t_k^2}\bigr)$
  \State $z^{k+1} = x^{k+1} + \tfrac{t_k-1}{t_{k+1}}(x^{k+1}-x^k)$
  \If{$\|x^{k+1}-x^k\|\le\code{tol}\,\|x^{k+1}\|$} \textbf{stop} \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $x^{k+1}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Image quality metrics}

\code{psnr(x, x\_ref)} returns $20\log_{10}(\text{range}) -
10\log_{10}\text{MSE}$, with the range of the reference image
($\max-\min$) as the peak value. \code{ssim(x, x\_ref)} implements the
structural similarity index~\cite{WangBovikSheikhSimoncelli2004} with an
$11\times11$ uniform window and $K_1=0.01$, $K_2=0.03$; its values can
differ slightly from implementations that use a Gaussian window.

\section{Verification}\label{sec:verification}
% ================================================================== %

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Adjoints.} For random $x$ and $y$, the ratio
        $\langle Ax,y\rangle/\langle x,A^*y\rangle$ equals 1 to within
        $2\times10^{-15}$ for the MRI operator and for the CT operator with
        30, 60, and 180 angles (the tests check 17, 45, and 180 angles).
  \item \emph{Operator norms.} Power iteration gives $\|A\|^2 = 1$ to six
        digits for both operators.
  \item \emph{Noise model.} Unsampled k-space locations are exactly zero,
        and the empirical standard deviation of each noise component matches
        \code{noise\_std}.
  \item \emph{Wavelets.} For Haar, db4, and sym8 the transform preserves the
        norm and passes the dot-product test to $10^{-12}$.
  \item \emph{Solvers.} Chambolle--Pock on real CT data raises no complex
        warnings and returns a real image.
  \item \emph{Golden outputs.} \code{tests/test\_golden.py} reruns the quick
        configuration of both examples and compares the PSNR and SSIM of
        every method with stored values (\code{tests/golden/quick\_examples.json}).
\end{itemize}
The test suite has 60 tests. Continuous integration runs it on Python 3.10
to 3.13, runs both examples in quick mode from outside the repository, and
executes the tutorial.

\subsection{The examples}

$256\times256$ Shepp--Logan phantom from 25\% variable-density sampling
(4$\times$ acceleration) at a data SNR of 30\,dB. The CT example,
\code{examples/02\_ct\_sparse\_view.py}, uses 45 parallel-beam views with Gaussian noise of 1\% of the sinogram RMS (40\,dB).
The regularization parameters were chosen by a coarse search for the best

\subsection{Further studies}

The tutorial adds three studies at $128\times128$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Parameter choice for MRI.} The discrepancy
        principle~\cite{Morozov1966}, which chooses the TV parameter whose
        residual $\|Ax_\alpha-y\|$ matches the noise norm
        $\sqrt{2m}\,\sigma$ ($m$ sampled locations), selects
        $\alpha=5.6\times10^{-3}$ (36.2\,dB), against the best value
        $3.2\times10^{-3}$ (37.5\,dB).
  \item \emph{Number of CT views.} With $\alpha$ retuned for each case, TV
        reaches 22.9, 27.3, 30.2, and 30.6\,dB with 15, 30, 60, and 120
        views; the best $\alpha$ falls from $3\times10^{-3}$ to $10^{-4}$.
  \item \emph{A natural image.} On the \code{camera} image from
        scikit-image (MRI, same mask and noise), zero-filling gives 23.2\,dB,
        TV ($\alpha=3\times10^{-3}$) 30.2\,dB, and Haar CS
        ($\lambda=3\times10^{-3}$) 27.1\,dB; db4 wavelets give about 24\,dB.
        The gain from TV is about 7\,dB, against 17\,dB on the piecewise
        constant phantom.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Run times}

On an Apple M-series laptop with Python 3.13, applying $A$ and then $A^*$
to a $256\times256$ image takes 1.0\,ms for MRI and 4.8\,ms (45 views) or
20.9\,ms (180 views) for CT. Building the CT matrix and its norm takes
0.25\,s and 1.0\,s for these two cases (4.6 and 18.4 million nonzeros), once
per image size and set of angles. The full MRI example runs in about 5\,s and
the full CT example in about 20\,s; the version~0.1 CT example took about
eight minutes.

\section{Choosing the parameters}\label{sec:parameters}
% ================================================================== %

\paragraph{Noise.} Specify the MRI noise through the data SNR with
\eqref{eq:snr}; 30\,dB is a moderate level. For CT, the examples add
Gaussian noise relative to the sinogram RMS; real CT noise is Poisson and
depends on the dose (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{Regularization parameters.} With $\|A\|=1$ and images in
$[0,1]$, useful values lie roughly between $10^{-4}$ and $10^{-2}$ for all
three methods. The best value grows with the noise level and falls as the
number of measurements grows (Section~\ref{sec:results}). Without the true
image:
\begin{itemize}
  \item for MRI the model is exact, so the misfit is noise and the
        discrepancy principle works well when the noise level is known, for
        example from a noise-only scan;
  \item otherwise, sweep the parameter on a logarithmic grid and choose
        where the image stops changing qualitatively; check both PSNR-like
        and structural measures, which can disagree.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Iterations.} Chambolle--Pock has no stopping test: 500
iterations suffice at $128\times128$ and 1000 to 2000 at $256\times256$ for
the examples. FISTA usually stops earlier on its relative-change test; CG
needs at most a few hundred iterations.

\paragraph{Your own operators.} Any pair \code{A}, \code{At} works. Run the
dot-product test first, and either normalize the operator to unit norm or
pass \code{L}; otherwise the solvers estimate $\|A\|^2$ by power iteration
and the regularization parameters must be rescaled by it.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
% ================================================================== %

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{MRI physics.} Single-coil Fourier encoding of a real image:
        no coil sensitivities (parallel imaging), no image phase (the adjoint
        returns the real part), and no non-Cartesian trajectories beyond what
        a custom mask can express.
  \item \emph{Sampling.} The built-in mask samples individual k-space points
        outside the calibration band, which suits 3-D acquisitions; for
        single-slice 2-D MRI, build a mask of whole lines.
  \item \emph{CT physics.} Two-dimensional parallel-beam geometry on the
        inscribed circle; no fan or cone beam, no Poisson noise model, no
        beam hardening.
  \item \emph{Algorithms.} Chambolle--Pock runs a fixed number of
        iterations; the TV gradient uses periodic boundaries; the wavelet
        transform is orthogonal only for orthogonal wavelets and image sides
        divisible by $2^{\text{level}}$.
  \item \emph{Methods.} Classical variational methods only; no learned
        reconstruction and no GPU support.
\end{itemize}
